# SweetDrop China: Speech Script (9 slides, ~14 min)

Short sentences and round numbers, so the text is easy to learn. Speaking pace is about 130 words per minute.

**Speaker split:** A: slides 1–2 · B: slides 3–4 · C: slides 5–6 · D: slides 7–9
The *italic lines* at the end of each part are hand-overs to the next speaker.

---

## Slide 1: The idea (Speaker A, ~1:45)

Good afternoon. We are SweetDrop, in Chinese 甘点, Gān Diǎn.

Imagine a warm cookie the size of your hand. Ten minutes ago, you designed it yourself on your phone. You chose the dough, the filling and the topping. You even wrote your friend's name on top. That is our product.

Why Shanghai? Because young people there don't just buy food. They buy an experience they can post. The Chinese call it 颜值经济, the "looks economy": a product must look good on camera first. And they love 打卡, visiting a trendy place and checking in online.

One example. The Wukang Road and Anfu Road area in Shanghai has no big shopping mall. But according to the city government, it gets 30 to 40 thousand visitors every day. Most of them are young, and most of them are holding a phone.

The market is big too. China's bakery market was about 611 billion yuan in 2024, and it grows around 7 percent a year. And the average Chinese person still spends about seven times less on bakery than a Japanese person. So there is a lot of room to grow.

Today we are asking you for 1.9 million yuan, about 284 thousand dollars, for 30 percent of the company.

---

## Slide 2: Product and competitors (Speaker A, ~1:30)

So how does it work? In our WeChat Mini-Program, you build your cookie in four steps: base, filling, topping and message. We bake it when you order, so it is always warm.

Every Monday we also launch four to six limited flavours. Some exist only in China: matcha, taro, black sesame, even durian.

We changed the recipe for Chinese taste. Our cookies have 30 percent less sugar, because "too sweet" is the most common complaint about Western desserts in China. For holidays like 520, Qixi, Mid-Autumn and Chinese New Year, we sell gift boxes with personal designs.

A cookie costs 29 to 32 yuan, about four and a half dollars. That's the same as Crumbl in the US.

What about competitors? There are cookie shops in Shanghai: Strictly Cookies, CookieYak!, and Princess Su on Nanjing Road. There are premium bakeries like Holiland. But they all sell ready-made cookies. Nobody offers a hot cookie made to your own design. Our real competitor is the cup of milk tea, the 20-to-40-yuan treat people buy every afternoon.

*Now [Name B] will explain the biggest risk in our model.*

---

## Slide 3: Lesson from Crumbl (Speaker B, ~1:30)

Thank you. Our idea is inspired by Crumbl Cookies, a very successful American chain. But we have to be honest about Crumbl's problem.

In 2022, an average Crumbl store made 1.84 million dollars a year. In 2025, it made only 1.14 million. That's a drop of almost 40 percent in three years.

Why? Crumbl's main idea is "new flavour every week". People come to try it once, then they get bored. Novelty brings people in, but it does not create a habit.

So we built three protections.

First, personal occasions. A cookie with your friend's name is a birthday gift or an apology, not a trend. People always have reasons to celebrate.

Second, the holiday calendar. Chinese gift-giving holidays come back every year, and they bring big sales peaks.

Third, co-creation. The best cookies designed by customers become next week's flavour, with the customer's name on the menu.

And to stay realistic, our financial plan already assumes that sales fall by 15 percent after the opening hype.

---

## Slide 4: Entering China (Speaker B, ~1:30)

Now, how do we legally enter China? This builds on our first project about China's political, economic and legal environment.

We register a WFOE, a wholly foreign-owned enterprise. A joint venture would be cheaper, but a WFOE lets us fully control our recipes and our brand. Under the new Company Law from 2024, we must pay in our capital within five years. Our capital is 1.9 million yuan, the same as our ask.

Before opening, we need a food business licence, 食品经营许可证. The local market regulator, SAMR, gives it after inspecting the shop.

Our Mini-Program also needs an official ICP filing, and customer data must follow China's personal information law, PIPL.

Taxes are good for small companies. As a "small low-profit enterprise", we pay only 5 percent profit tax until the end of 2027.

But taking money out of China is slow. First an audit, then 10 percent of profit goes to a required reserve, then the tax on dividends, 5 to 10 percent. We plan for this from day one.

*Now [Name C] will show where we open and how we work.*

---

## Slide 5: Location and logistics (Speaker C, ~1:45)

Thank you. We compared two locations.

Option one: a shop in a mall on Nanjing West Road. Great traffic, but very expensive. According to Cushman & Wakefield, rent there is about 2,250 yuan per square metre per month. For our 50 square metres, that is more than 110 thousand yuan a month. With that rent, we would lose money.

Option two: a street shop on Wukang and Anfu Road. Rent is about 45 thousand a month, less than half. The people walking past are exactly our customers. And a queue outside a street shop is free advertising. Also, rents in Shanghai are falling right now, so we can negotiate.

So we choose Wukang–Anfu.

How do we operate? We don't build a factory for one shop. We rent space in a shared, licensed kitchen. There we make the dough every day. At 6:30 in the morning, a cold van brings it to the shop. In the shop, we only bake and decorate. One tray takes about 12 minutes.

For delivery, we use Meituan and Ele.me, China's biggest delivery apps, within 3 kilometres and 30 minutes. Gift boxes go by SF Express across Shanghai and nearby cities.

---

## Slide 6: Marketing (Speaker C, ~1:30)

Marketing in China starts with content.

Two months before opening, we send free custom cookies to 100 small bloggers on Xiaohongshu, China's lifestyle app. Small bloggers are cheaper than big influencers, and people trust them more. On Douyin, the Chinese TikTok, people vote for our opening flavour. So the audience is part of the launch from the start.

On opening day, the first thousand people who join our Mini-Program get a free message on their cookie.

But here is the key point. Delivery apps bring customers, but they take around 20 percent of every order. So our goal is to move customers into our own app. Members get a stamp card, a free cookie on their birthday and early access to new flavours.

We also run big campaigns on Chinese holidays, and we sell logo cookies to offices nearby as corporate gifts.

Our budget is 150 thousand yuan for the launch and 30 thousand a month after that.

*Now [Name D] will take you through the numbers.*

---

## Slide 7: Finance (Speaker D, ~2:00)

Thank you. Let's start with one cookie.

On average, one cookie brings us about 29 yuan without VAT. After ingredients, packaging and delivery fees, we keep 16 yuan. That's 56 percent before rent and salaries.

Orders in the shop are the most profitable: we keep two-thirds. On delivery we keep only about 40 percent, because of the platform fee. That's why we push our own app.

We break even at about 165 orders a day. Our plan is about 200.

Now the full first year. In our base case, the shop sells about 180 thousand cookies. Revenue is 5.2 million yuan, about 780 thousand dollars. That's still lower than an average Crumbl store in the US. Our EBITDA is about 590 thousand yuan, or 11 percent. We get our invested money back in about two and a half years.

We also tested a pessimistic case: 35 percent fewer customers and higher costs. Then we lose about 320 thousand yuan in the first year. But we keep a 400-thousand reserve exactly for this. At month nine, we decide: fix the model, or switch to delivery and gifts only.

---

## Slide 8: Risks (Speaker D, ~1:15)

Every business has risks. Here are our five biggest, and our answer to each.

One: copycats. In China, someone will copy us within months. So we register our brand and our Chinese name before we open, and we change the menu every week.

Two: the trend can fade, like Crumbl. Every month we check how many customers come back. If too few return, we focus on gifts and corporate orders and cut costs.

Three: rent. We sign a three-year lease with a limit on rent increases. And about a third of our orders come from delivery, which doesn't depend on foot traffic.

Four: regulation. We start in a kitchen that is already licensed, with clear food-safety rules.

Five: dependence on delivery apps. They can raise fees at any time. So every month we move more customers into our own app.

---

## Slide 9: Benefits and the ask (Speaker D, ~1:15)

What does Shanghai get from us? About 10 new local jobs, all paid above the city's minimum wage of 2,740 yuan. About 1.8 million yuan a year goes to local suppliers. And around 300 to 400 thousand yuan goes to taxes and social contributions.

What do we get? A tested business model and our own customer base, ready for a second shop.

So here is our ask: 1.9 million yuan, about 284 thousand dollars, for 30 percent of SweetDrop China.

We suggest two tranches: 1.5 million now, and the 400-thousand reserve after month four, if we hit our targets.

Our first-year targets are 5.2 million yuan in revenue, about 200 orders a day and 25 thousand app members.

Prove one shop, then open the next. Thank you! We're happy to answer your questions.

---

## Likely questions: short answers to learn

- **"Why not a joint venture?"** It's cheaper, but we would share control of recipes and brand. Our brand is our main asset.
- **"Why will people pay 30 yuan for a cookie?"** They already pay 20–40 yuan for milk tea and premium bakery. We give them a personal product they can post and gift.
- **"What if sales are much lower?"** That's our pessimistic case. The loss is 320 thousand, our reserve is 400 thousand, and we have a decision point at month nine.
- **"Where do the numbers come from?"** Market data from iiMedia and USDA, rent from Cushman & Wakefield and JLL, Crumbl data from its official franchise documents. Our prices and volumes are our own assumptions, based on these sources.
- **"How do you get profit out of China?"** Audit, 10 percent reserve, then dividends with a 5–10 percent withholding tax. We plan to reinvest year-one profit into a second shop anyway.
