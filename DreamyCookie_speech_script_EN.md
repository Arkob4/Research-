# Dreamy Cookie China: Speech Script (~13 minutes)

Speakers: A — slides 1–2 · B — slides 3–4 · C — slides 5–6 · D — slides 7–10
Exchange rate: US$1 = ¥6.7. Sources are listed at the end.

---

## Slide 1. The idea (A, ~0:45)

Hi everyone! We are Dreamy Cookie, in Chinese 梦幻曲奇. We sell warm giant cookies that customers design themselves: they pick the base, the filling, the topping, and add a name or a message.

Why Shanghai? Young people there pay for experiences and great photos, and queuing at a trendy spot is already a reason to post. China's bakery market is worth 611 billion yuan and grows about 7% a year.

We are asking for 1.9 million yuan for 30% of the company.

---

## Slide 2. Product, price and competitors (A, ~1:45)

Here's how it works. In the store or right in Meituan, customers build their own cookie, and we bake it to order, so it's always warm. Every week we launch new flavours like matcha, taro, black sesame and berry cheesecake. We use 30% less sugar than the American recipe, because Chinese customers don't like desserts that are too sweet. For 520, Qixi, Mid-Autumn and Chinese New Year we sell gift boxes.

We set our prices against Shanghai competitors. Our main benchmark is **Butterful & Creamorous, or B&C**: a Korean premium bakery brand that came to Shanghai in 2021 through Xintiandi and now runs more than 20 of its own stores. Regular items cost 9 to 29 yuan, hits 35 to 58 yuan, and the average ticket is about 70 yuan. People queue up to three hours for their signature croissant, and resellers sell it for twice the price. B&C proved that Shanghai will queue and pay for premium bakery.

Other market prices: Holiland's average ticket is 40 to 60 yuan, the Shanghai cookie shop CookieYak! about 43 yuan, and a Kumo Kumo cheesecake costs 39 yuan.

Our prices: a classic cookie is 25 yuan, a custom one from 32, and premium fillings add 3 to 6 yuan. We're cheaper than B&C's hits but in the same premium segment. An order of two cookies is around 55 to 60 yuan.

What makes us different? B&C, Holiland and local cookie shops sell ready-made pastries from a display case. Nobody in Shanghai makes a warm cookie to your own design.

*Over to [name B].*

## Slide 3. Why now: Crumbl isn't in China yet (B, ~1:15)

We didn't invent this format. The American chain Crumbl Cookies grew to more than 1,100 stores in seven years, with sales above 1.2 billion dollars a year, largely thanks to TikTok. It only operates in the US and Canada. Its first international deals are with Mexico, and Australia and the UAE are planned. China is not on that list.

So the spot is free, and we want to take it first. But we won't copy one to one. The Chinese market gets tired of trendy brands quickly: Kumo Kumo had more than 200 stores at its peak and is now closing them. So we have three differences. First, customisation: a cookie with a name is bought as a gift, not just for the trend. Second, local taste: less sugar and Chinese ingredients. Third, holiday gifting that sells every year.

---

## Slide 4. Entering China (B, ~1:30)

We register a WFOE, a wholly foreign-owned enterprise. A joint venture would be cheaper, but we'd have to share our recipes and brand. Under the 2024 Company Law, registered capital must be paid in within five years. Ours is 1.9 million yuan, exactly our ask.

We need a food business licence, 食品经营许可证. The market regulator SAMR issues it after inspecting the premises, separately for each address: one for the pop-up and one for the store. We register the brand and the Chinese name as trademarks before opening.

Taxes for small businesses are low: 5% corporate tax and 1% VAT, as long as yearly revenue stays under 5 million yuan. Both breaks run until the end of 2027.

How do we get profit out? The yuan isn't freely convertible: every outbound payment is checked by the bank under the rules of SAFE, the foreign exchange regulator. But it's not banned — there are legal channels.

Channel one is dividends. They're paid once a year, after an audit and after putting 10% of profit into a statutory reserve. The tax on them is 10%, or 5% under a double-tax treaty.

Channel two is royalties. The parent company owns the brand and recipes, and the Chinese company pays it a licence fee every quarter. Royalties reduce Chinese corporate tax, but they carry 10% withholding tax and 6% VAT, and the price must be at market level.

Payments above 50,000 dollars need a tax filing. We plan both channels from day one so the money doesn't get stuck.

*Over to [name C].*

## Slide 5. Location: pop-up first, then a store (C, ~1:45)

We won't sign a three-year lease right away. We start with a pop-up, a temporary spot for two months, and only then open a permanent store.

Why? Trendy brands in China close as fast as they open. According to industry data, the number of bakeries in China fell from about 340,000 to 298,000 in a single year. A pop-up lets us test real demand, flavours and prices in two months without the risk of a long lease. Many brands do this: the coffee brand Saturnbird, for example, ran six-month temporary stores precisely to test its store model.

For the pop-up we chose **TX Huaihai, the "Youth Energy Center"** on Huaihai Road. It's China's first mall designed as an exhibition space and one of Shanghai's main youth hangouts, with constant pop-ups from trendy brands. Pop-up space in Shanghai malls costs about 7 to 10 thousand yuan a day for an atrium spot. We take a small spot of about 25 square metres and budget around 5,000 yuan a day, or 150,000 a month.

After two months we open a permanent store in the Wukang–Anfu area, at the other end of Huaihai Road, a few kilometres from TX Huaihai. It has about 180 street shops and 30 to 40 thousand visitors a day, and in the first quarter of 2025 alone it hosted more than 10 pop-ups. Rent for 60 square metres is about 57,000 yuan a month. The same space in a Nanjing West Road mall would cost more than 130,000.

We make the dough right in the store, so we don't need a separate factory. We need two ovens, a mixer and fridges, and we move them from the pop-up to the store. Meituan and Ele.me couriers deliver within 3 kilometres, and SF Express ships gift boxes.

## Slide 6. Marketing: platforms and vouchers (C, ~1:30)

We don't build our own app or our own delivery, because customers already live on Meituan, Ele.me and Douyin. We go where they are.

Our main tool is vouchers:
- **Meituan and Ele.me:** about 15% off delivery orders, at our expense.
- **Douyin:** we sell vouchers, for example two cookies for 49 yuan instead of 62. The customer buys the voucher in a video and comes to the store with it. The platform takes a small fee.

Vouchers lower our price, and that's already built into the financials.

The launch budget is 150,000 yuan:
- about 60,000 for free cookies and fees for 100 small bloggers on Xiaohongshu before the pop-up opens;
- 50,000 for Douyin and Xiaohongshu ads;
- 25,000 for the opening event;
- 15,000 for photo and video.

After that, 30,000 a month:
- 12,000 for promotion inside Meituan and Ele.me;
- 10,000 for Douyin ads;
- 8,000 for new bloggers.

## Slide 7. Unit economics: one cookie and one month (D, ~1:15)

**One cookie.** When a customer buys in the store for 31 yuan, after ingredients, packaging and payment fees we keep about 20 yuan — two-thirds of the price. With a Douyin voucher we keep 13 yuan. On delivery we keep 10: the platform with delivery takes about 22%, plus our discount. On average every cookie brings about 16 yuan before rent and wages.

**Store fixed costs per month:**
- rent — 57,000;
- wages — 65,000: a manager, two bakers, two cashiers and part-timers. This already includes 30% social contributions, and all wages are above the Shanghai minimum wage of 2,740 yuan;
- marketing — 30,000;
- utilities — 8,000;
- accounting and other — 8,000.

That's about 170,000 a month. To break even, the store needs to sell about 355 cookies a day.

## Slide 8. Financials: three scenarios (D, ~1:15)

For comparison with competitors: a typical 100-square-metre bakery, according to Huaan Securities, makes 270,000 yuan a month with about 9% profit. B&C says it makes 70 to 100 thousand yuan a day per store. An average US Crumbl makes about 7.6 million yuan a year. Our targets sit below the leaders.

**Three first-year scenarios** (two months of pop-up and ten months of the store):
- **Bad:** 280 cookies a day. Revenue 3.2 million yuan and an operating loss of 420,000, covered by our 500,000 reserve.
- **Base:** 450 cookies a day. Revenue 5.1 million. Out of that: VAT 156,000; ingredients, packaging and platform fees 2.2 million; rent 870,000; wages 780,000; marketing 360,000; other about 190,000. EBITDA is 575,000, or 11.6%, close to an average bakery. After depreciation and 5% corporate tax, net profit is 420,000. The investment pays back in about two years.
- **Good:** 700 cookies a day, roughly the level of an average US Crumbl. Revenue 8 million, net profit 1.9 million, payback in about seven months.

---

## Slide 9. Risks (D, ~1:15)

We see five main risks.
- **Hype fades fast.** That happened to Kumo Kumo, and China lost about 40,000 bakeries in a year. Our answer: pop-up first, lease second. If the store sells fewer than 355 cookies a day for three months in a row, we cut costs or switch to delivery only.
- **Platforms and voucher wars.** With promotions, platforms take 25% or more from some sellers. We cap discounts at 15% and keep at least half of sales in the store itself.
- **Copycats.** Even B&C got copied. We register the trademark before launch and refresh the menu every week.
- **Licences and food safety.** Every address needs its own licence. We choose premises approved for food production and write kitchen standards before opening.
- **Getting money out.** We use only legal channels — dividends and royalties — leave time for the bank's review and prepare the paperwork in advance.

---

## Slide 10. Summary and the ask (D, ~1:00)

To sum up:
- **Why now:** Crumbl isn't in China yet, and B&C proved that Shanghai queues for premium bakery.
- **How we lower risk:** a pop-up instead of a long lease from day one, dough made in the store, and sales through platforms where customers already are.
- **What Shanghai gets:** 7 to 8 jobs, about 1.8 million yuan a year for local suppliers, and around 350,000 yuan in taxes and contributions.

We're asking for 1.9 million yuan, about 284,000 dollars, for 30% of Dreamy Cookie China. About 0.85 million goes to fit-out, equipment, the pop-up stand and the rent deposit; about 0.55 million to registration, team training, launch marketing and contingency; and 0.5 million is a reserve for the bad case.

In the base case you get your money back in two years, in the good case in under a year, and in the bad case the reserve covers the loss.

We want to be the first in China selling cookies that customers design themselves. Thank you — we're happy to take your questions!
